## FW01: On guard (En garde)

The base stance: heels in line 1.5–2 foot lengths apart, front foot at the opponent, rear foot at about 90°, knees over the toes, torso upright and bladed, weapon hand at chest height with the tip on the upper chest.

Before every action and as the neutral state you return to after each phrase.

1. Place the heels on one line, front foot pointing at the opponent, rear foot turned out.
2. Sink with both knees tracking over the toes; keep the pelvis level.
3. Settle the weapon elbow a hand-width in front of the flank, hand at chest height in sixte.
4. Aim the point at the opponent's upper chest and relax the rear arm.

Common errors:

- Knees collapse inward, so every step leaks power and balance.
- Stance too narrow, so the lunge has no base and the head bobs.
- Hand held high and wide, which opens the low line and slows parries.
- Weight on the front foot, so the retreat starts late.

Tactics: A compact guard keeps every parry within 10–15 cm of hand travel and hides your intention.

Right of way: Standing on guard carries no priority. Priority starts only with a threatening extension.

Countered by: Foundation technique.

## FW02: Advance (Marche)

The front foot moves first, toe lifting and heel landing, and the rear foot follows the same distance.

To close distance, set up an attack, or push the opponent toward the end line.

1. Lift the front toe and reach forward from the knee, not the hip.
2. Land heel first, keeping the head level.
3. Bring the rear foot the same distance to restore the stance.

Common errors:

- Rear foot moves first, telegraphing the step.
- Feet drag together, shrinking the stance.
- Bouncing step that lifts the head several centimetres.

Tactics: Vary the length and rhythm of your advances; regular steps invite an attack on preparation.

Right of way: An advance without an extending arm is preparation: the opponent may attack into it.

Countered by: CT04 (reliable): An advance without extension is preparation and can be attacked.; CT01 (situational): A stop hit lands while the foot is in the air.

## FW03: Retreat (Retraite)

The rear foot moves first and the front foot follows, keeping the stance width.

To keep the attacker out of distance, draw a short attack, or buy time to parry.

1. Push from the front foot and reach back with the rear foot.
2. Land the rear foot and let the front foot follow the same distance.
3. Keep the blade still in guard while the feet move.

Common errors:

- Front foot leaves first, so the weight tips backward.
- Steps too long to repeat quickly.
- Blade drops while retreating, opening the high line.

Tactics: A retreat that matches the attacker's rhythm makes the final fall short; break the rhythm to set a trap.

Right of way: Making an attack fall short passes priority to you, provided you then act.

Countered by: FW13 (reliable): The advance-lunge covers the extra distance the retreat gains.; FW15 (situational): The flèche out-runs a single retreat.; FW22 (situational): Stealing distance removes the space the retreat relies on.

## FW04: Lunge (Fente)

Arm-first lunge: the arm extends, the front foot kicks forward toe-up and lands heel first with the shin vertical, the rear leg drives straight and the rear foot stays flat.

The finishing footwork for most attacks from lunge distance.

1. Start the arm extension so the point threatens first.
2. Kick the front foot forward toe-up, low over the floor.
3. Drive from the rear leg until it is straight, rear foot flat.
4. Land heel first with the shin vertical and the knee over the instep.
5. Drop the rear arm parallel to the rear leg; keep the torso lean under 15°.

Common errors:

- The front foot leads the arm, so you lose priority.
- Knee travels past the toes, overloading the joint and slowing the recovery.
- Rear foot rolls onto its side, losing the drive.
- Torso dives forward, so the riposte catches you off balance.

Tactics: The lunge is fast but commits you; keep the recovery ready and attack only at the right measure.

Right of way: Priority starts with the arm extension and holds while the extension is progressive.

Countered by: PA01 (reliable): A lateral parry closing the line catches a straight lunge.; PA02 (reliable): A sixte parry closes the outside high line the lunge finishes in.; CT11 (reliable): Pulling distance makes the lunge fall short.; CT01 (risky): Only works if the preparation is slow; otherwise the attack has priority.

## FW05: Recovery backward (Retour en garde en arrière)

From the lunge, the rear leg bends and pulls the body back while the front foot pushes off, returning to guard.

After a missed or parried lunge to re-establish distance and defence.

1. Bend the rear knee to pull the hips back.
2. Push off the front heel and bring the front foot back.
3. Return the blade to a covering position as the guard re-forms.

Common errors:

- Recovering with the arm still extended and the line open.
- Standing up tall instead of staying low.
- Slow push from the front heel, leaving you in range of the riposte.

Tactics: A fast recovery denies the delayed riposte and resets the measure.

Right of way: Recovering gives no priority; the opponent who parried still owns the riposte.

Countered by: RR05 (situational): A delayed riposte waits for the recovery and hits as you return.; RR10 (situational): Redoublement pressure catches the recovering fencer.

## FW06: Recovery forward (Retour en garde en avant)

From the lunge, the rear foot comes up to guard so you keep the ground you gained.

When the opponent retreated and you want to keep pressure.

1. Keep the front foot planted.
2. Bring the rear foot forward to restore stance width.
3. Rise to guard height with the blade covering.

Common errors:

- Dragging the rear foot so the stance is too narrow.
- Blade left extended, inviting a beat.
- Rising too high, which slows the next action.

Tactics: Use it to chase a retreating opponent, but expect an attack while you close.

Right of way: The forward recovery is preparation; the opponent may attack into it.

Countered by: CT04 (reliable): The forward recovery is preparation and can be attacked.

## FW07: Half-advance (Demi-marche)

A partial front-foot step that tests reactions or draws an action without committing distance.

To probe whether the opponent retreats, parries, or attacks into your footwork.

1. Lift and place the front foot a short distance forward.
2. Keep the rear foot ready to follow or not.
3. Read the reaction before choosing the next action.

Common errors:

- Committing weight forward, so you cannot react.
- Repeating the same rhythm until it becomes a cue.
- Moving the blade with the foot.

Tactics: Draws an attack you can parry or makes the opponent reveal their distance habits.

Right of way: A check step without extension is preparation.

Countered by: FW25 (situational): Matching distance leaves the half-step with nothing to read.; CT03 (reliable): A point in line stops the probe from coming closer.

## FW08: Half-retreat (Demi-retraite)

A partial retreat used to draw the attack just short.

Against an attacker who commits early to a fixed distance.

1. Move the rear foot back a short distance.
2. Leave the front foot in place or follow minimally.
3. Prepare the parry or counterattack as the attack falls short.

Common errors:

- Too small to change the measure.
- Leaning back instead of stepping.
- Not ready to act once the attack falls short.

Tactics: Small distance changes are hard to see and ruin the attacker's timing.

Right of way: If the attack falls short, priority passes to you.

Countered by: FW22 (situational): Stealing distance gives the attacker back the centimetres.; AT15 (situational): A broken-time attack waits through the half-retreat.

## FW09: Double advance (Double marche)

Two advances with an accelerating rhythm.

To gain ground quickly and arrive at lunge distance with momentum.

1. Take a measured first advance.
2. Accelerate into a shorter, faster second advance.
3. Arrive balanced and ready to lunge.

Common errors:

- Equal rhythm, which is easy to time.
- Overstriding the second step.
- Arm drifting forward without a real threat.

Tactics: Accelerating feet compress the opponent's reaction time.

Right of way: Footwork without extension remains preparation.

Countered by: CT04 (reliable): Two steps of preparation give two chances to attack into them.

## FW10: Jump advance (Saut en avant)

Both feet leave the floor low and land together.

To change rhythm or set up a balestra.

1. Push from both legs, keeping the jump low.
2. Travel forward with the stance unchanged.
3. Land both feet together, knees bent.

Common errors:

- Jumping high, so the head rises far above guard height.
- Landing on the heels and losing spring.
- Arm moving with the jump.

Tactics: The airborne moment cannot be changed; use it only when the opponent is not ready.

Right of way: Preparation; the opponent may attack while you are in the air.

Countered by: CT04 (reliable): You cannot react in the air; an attack on preparation lands.

## FW12: Balestra (Balestra)

A jump advance straight into a lunge, landing with a stamp.

To surprise with a sudden change of rhythm from advance-lunge distance.

1. Extend the arm as the jump starts.
2. Land both feet with a stamp.
3. Launch the lunge immediately from the landing.

Common errors:

- Arm stays bent through the jump, so the attack has no priority.
- Jump too high and slow.
- Pause between landing and lunge.

Tactics: The stamp and rhythm change freeze the opponent for a moment.

Right of way: With the arm extending from the jump, it is an attack with priority.

## FW13: Advance-lunge (Marche-fente)

An advance chained into a lunge with no pause.

From advance-lunge distance against an opponent who holds ground.

1. Start the advance with the arm beginning to extend.
2. Land the rear foot and launch the lunge in one rhythm.
3. Finish with the arm fully extended as the front foot lands.

Common errors:

- Pause between the advance and the lunge.
- Arm extended too early and held, inviting a beat.
- Advance too long to chain.

Tactics: The standard long attack; vary the advance to disguise the moment of the lunge.

Right of way: If the arm extends progressively through the advance, priority is held throughout.

Countered by: CT04 (reliable): The advance is preparation if the arm is not extending.; PA01 (situational): A parry in retreat catches the end of the long attack.

## FW14: Patinando (Patinando)

A short, quick advance; the rear foot snaps up and the lunge launches as the rear foot lands.

To accelerate into an attack from slightly beyond lunge distance.

1. Take a short, sharp front-foot step.
2. Snap the rear foot up.
3. Launch the lunge as the rear foot lands, arm leading.

Common errors:

- Rear foot lands heavy, so the lunge starts late.
- Step too long, losing the snap.
- Arm lags behind the feet.

Tactics: The snap of the rear foot is the acceleration; keep the front step small.

Right of way: An attack with priority once the arm extends with the forward movement.

Countered by: CT03 (reliable): A point in line established early beats an attack that does not remove it.; CT11 (situational): Distance pull leaves the patinando short.

## FW15: Flèche (Flèche)

A running attack: arm extends, weight shifts over the front foot, the front leg drives, the rear leg passes, and the tip lands before the passing foot.

Against a static opponent from slightly beyond lunge distance, or to surprise.

1. Extend the arm first.
2. Shift the weight over the front foot.
3. Drive from the front leg as the rear leg swings past.
4. Land the touch before the passing foot touches down.
5. Run past the opponent's side without contact.

Common errors:

- Rear leg passes before the arm extends, so priority is lost.
- Hit lands after the passing foot, making the attack slow.
- Running into the opponent: corps-à-corps.

Tactics: Fully committing; there is no recovery, so use it when the distance and timing are certain.

Right of way: An attack with priority; body contact halts the phrase with no touch.

Countered by: PA01 (situational): A retreat plus quarte parry catches the flèche.; PA02 (situational): A retreat plus sixte parry catches the flèche.; CT02 (reliable): A time hit with opposition closes the line the flèche comes in.

## FW16: Cross-over forward (Passe avant)

The rear foot passes the front, then the front steps forward to restore guard.

To gain ground quickly in a long pursuit.

- Squaring the hips so the target widens.

## FW18: Appel (Appel)

A front-foot stamp used as a feint or distraction.

To provoke a reaction or mask the start of an attack.

1. Lift the front foot slightly.
2. Stamp sharply in place.
3. Watch the reaction and act on it.

Common errors:

- Stamping so hard you lose balance.
- Leaning forward with the stamp.
- Using it without a plan for the reaction.

Tactics: Works on nervous opponents; experienced ones ignore it.

Right of way: Preparation; it carries no priority.

Countered by: CT01 (situational): Ignore the stamp and stop-hit into it.

## FW20: Broken-time step (Marche en temps rompu)

An advance with a deliberate mid-step pause.

To disrupt an opponent who times your steps.

1. Start a normal advance.
2. Freeze for a fraction of a tempo mid-step.
3. Complete the step and continue.

Common errors:

- Pause too long, gifting a tempo.
- Rocking the head during the pause.
- No follow-up action prepared.

Tactics: Changes the rhythm the opponent is reading.

Right of way: Preparation; the pause invites an attack into it.

Countered by: CT04 (reliable): Attack into the pause.

## FW21: Rhythm change (Changement de rythme)

Common errors:

## FW22: Stealing distance (Vol de distance)

A creeping foot gains measure without a visible step.

Before an attack, to move from out of distance to lunge distance unseen.

1. Slide the rear foot forward a few centimetres.
2. Slide the front foot to restore stance.
3. Keep the upper body and blade perfectly still.

Common errors:

- Upper body moves, revealing the gain.
- Stance shrinks.
- Gaining too much in one slide.

Tactics: Invisible distance is the best preparation for a simple attack.

Right of way: Preparation, but hard to see and so hard to punish.

Countered by: FW03 (reliable): A retreat restores the measure.; CT03 (situational): A point in line makes creeping in dangerous.

## FW25: Matching distance (Garder la distance)

Shadow the opponent's footwork to hold the measure.

Against an opponent probing for distance.

1. Watch the opponent's front foot.
2. Mirror each step with an equal step.
3. Hold a constant measure.

Common errors:

- Reacting to feints with large steps.
- Drifting toward the end line.
- Letting the measure shrink gradually.

Tactics: Denies the opponent a reference; they must change rhythm to gain.

Right of way: No priority; neutral footwork.

Countered by: FW21 (reliable): A rhythm change breaks the mirror.; FW07 (situational): A half-advance draws a reaction that reveals your timing.

## PA01: Quarte parry (Parade de quarte)

Inside high lateral parry: the hand moves 10–15 cm inside, supinated, point high.

Against attacks to the inside high line.

1. Move the hand 10–15 cm toward the line; the point moves more than the hand.
2. Meet the attacking faible with your forte.
3. Stop the parry crisply in position.
4. Riposte from where the parry ends.

Common errors:

- Wide parry that waves the arm instead of moving the hand.
- Parry with the faible, so the attack forces through.
- Parrying too early, so the attacker deceives it.
- No riposte, so the attacker can renew.

Tactics: Quarte parry is fast and economical; vary it with other parries so the attacker cannot plan a deception.

Right of way: A successful parry takes priority. The riposte keeps it if it starts within 1 T of the parry contact.
